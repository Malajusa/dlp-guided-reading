# NAPLAN-Informed Reading Question Design Standard

Use this reference when designing or reviewing Shared Reading and Guided Reading questions. It is informed by the supplied ACARA 2016 Year 5 Reading materials, but it is not a template for reproducing NAPLAN and does not turn Guided Reading into test preparation.

## Source basis

The design observations below were derived from these supplied source files:

- `e3-naplan-2016-final-test-reading-magazine-year-5.pdf` — stimulus texts and visuals;
- `e4-naplan-2016-final-test-reading-year-5.pdf` — 38 scored Reading questions plus practice questions;
- `naplan-2016-yr-5-paper-test-answers.pdf` — answer key, including the constructed-response note for Question 37.

The set includes short factual explanation, information text, narrative, persuasive signage with a functional visual, a more linguistically demanding literary extract, and paired reviews. Across those texts, questions move beyond retrieval into purpose, contextual meaning, character inference, language effect, persuasion, visual meaning, synthesis, and comparison of viewpoints.

## Core principle

Design the thinking demand first, then write the question. Difficulty should come from what evidence must be located, connected, interpreted, synthesised, or evaluated — not from unnecessarily difficult question wording.

Text difficulty and question difficulty are related but separate controls. A relatively accessible text can support demanding reasoning; a complex text does not justify a vague or poorly targeted question.

## Question taxonomy

Use the weekly Reading Focus to decide the dominant question type, while spiralling previously taught processes where useful.

| Type | Student thinking | Typical evidence demand |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Explicit retrieval | Locate directly stated information. | One sentence or clearly signposted detail. |
| Main idea / purpose | Decide what a paragraph or whole text is mainly doing. | Several details or the text as a whole. |
| Sequence / cause | Track order, reason, consequence, or change. | One or more connected events or propositions. |
| Vocabulary in context | Infer meaning or select a precise paraphrase from context. | Local sentence plus surrounding context. |
| Cohesion / reference | Resolve pronouns, substitutions, lexical links, or relationships between clauses. | Nearby sentence(s), sometimes across a paragraph boundary. |
| Inference | Combine textual clues to reach an unstated but defensible conclusion. | One or more clues; distance varies by group. |
| Characterisation | Infer trait, feeling, motive, relationship, or change from action and language. | Multiple narrative clues where appropriate. |
| Language effect | Explain what punctuation, imagery, figurative wording, sound, modality, or precise diction contributes. | Quoted or clearly located language plus context. |
| Author purpose / viewpoint | Infer why a choice was made or what position is being advanced. | Language, structure, content, or whole-text pattern. |
| Visual meaning | Explain information or persuasive meaning carried by an image, symbol, diagram, layout, caption, map, or table. | Visual alone or integrated visual + prose evidence. |
| Supported judgement | Select or construct the claim best supported by the text. | Competing plausible interpretations tested against evidence. |
| Synthesis | Combine evidence from separated parts of one text. | Multiple paragraphs or sections. |
| Cross-text comparison | Compare agreements, differences, evaluations, purposes, or perspectives across texts. | Evidence from two or more texts. |

Do not force every type into every session. Use a small set of high-value questions that reveal whether students achieved the weekly Learning Intention and Success Criteria.

## Evidence distance

Treat evidence distance as a deliberate difficulty control:

1. **Immediate** — answer is stated in the same sentence or an explicitly identified line.
2. **Local** — connect information across nearby sentences.
3. **Paragraph** — integrate information within a paragraph or short section.
4. **Distributed** — combine clues from separated paragraphs or sections.
5. **Whole text** — infer purpose, theme, viewpoint, development, or best-supported interpretation from the complete text.
6. **Cross-text** — reconcile, compare, or evaluate evidence from two or more texts.

Evidence distance is not a rigid year-level ladder. Choose the distance that fits the weekly focus and group profile, then provide enough textual support for a defensible answer.

## Multiple-choice questions

Use multiple choice when the instructional value comes from discriminating between plausible interpretations. Guided Reading should not inherit NAPLAN's test-heavy response balance; oral discussion and constructed responses remain central.

For a four-option multiple-choice question:

- write the correct answer from the evidence before writing distractors;
- keep all options grammatically compatible with the stem and broadly parallel in form;
- make every distractor plausible enough that a student must return to the text;
- keep options mutually distinct rather than offering near-synonyms with ambiguous boundaries;
- avoid joke answers, giveaway vocabulary, conspicuously longer correct answers, or repeated wording that points to the key;
- do not make a distractor false merely because it uses information absent from the passage if the question is supposed to test a finer distinction;
- where possible, make each distractor represent a recognisable reading error.

Useful distractor error models include:

- **surface match** — repeats a word or detail from the passage but does not answer the question;
- **partial evidence** — captures one clue while ignoring another decisive clue;
- **overgeneralisation** — extends a true detail beyond what the text supports;
- **wrong referent** — attaches a pronoun, action, opinion, or description to the wrong person or idea;
- **cause/effect reversal** — confuses reason and consequence;
- **literalisation** — treats figurative or implied language as purely literal;
- **unsupported inference** — sounds reasonable in the real world but is not supported by this text;
- **viewpoint confusion** — assigns one author's judgement to another or confuses author, narrator, character, and reader;
- **scope error** — chooses a detail when the question asks for whole-text purpose, or a broad claim when only a local detail is supported.

A distractor must not depend on trick wording. The distinction should be resolved by reading more accurately or reasoning more carefully.

## Constructed responses

Use constructed responses when students need to explain, justify, compare, synthesise, or expose their reasoning.

A strong constructed-response prompt makes the expected evidence relationship clear without giving away the answer. Depending on the group, responses may be oral, jointly rehearsed, short written answers, or more extended explanations.

Where evidence is required, the expected-answer record must distinguish:

- the conclusion or answer;
- the textual or visual evidence that supports it; and
- the reasoning that connects the evidence to the answer when that connection is not self-evident.

Do not reward unsupported opinion when the task is evidence-based. Accept alternative answers when the text genuinely supports more than one defensible interpretation.

## Group progression

Keep the same worthwhile weekly concept across Alpha-Epsilon. Adjust the access and reasoning demand rather than merely changing the number of questions.

- **Alpha:** favour immediate/local evidence, explicit referents, manageable option sets where MCQ is used, oral rehearsal, and prompts that make the evidence-search process visible.
- **Beta:** use local/paragraph evidence, nearby clue integration, and plausible distractors based on common partial readings.
- **Gamma:** expect independent evidence location at Year 4/5 demand, including paragraph/distributed evidence and explanation of why evidence supports an answer.
- **Delta:** increase distributed evidence, ambiguity, precision, competing plausible interpretations, synthesis, and evaluation.
- **Epsilon:** permit whole-text and cross-text reasoning, nuanced viewpoint, distal evidence, sophisticated paraphrase, ambiguity, and evaluation while preserving age-appropriate Year 4/5 subject matter.

Do not make Alpha childish or Epsilon dependent on obscure background knowledge. The intended distinction is reading access and reasoning complexity.

## NAPLAN observations worth retaining

The supplied 2016 set demonstrates several useful construction patterns:

- early factual items test precise retrieval as well as whole-text purpose;
- narrative items move from interpreting costume/action clues into emotion, character, figurative meaning, and whole-character judgement;
- the persuasive sign requires students to distinguish information, emotional appeal, supported claims, overall persuasive method, and the communicative purpose of its visual;
- the literary extract uses paraphrase and behaviour across paragraphs to test cautious inference, relationship change, and contextual meaning;
- the paired reviews require students to track which reviewer holds which view, find shared judgements, interpret review purpose, and compare evaluations across texts;
- incorrect options are usually related to the passage or task, so success depends on choosing the best-supported interpretation rather than spotting an absurd answer.

These are design observations from the supplied set, not claims about every NAPLAN Reading assessment.

## Question planning record

Before layout, record each substantive question in structured form. At minimum include:

- `id`;
- `question_type` from the taxonomy above;
- `target_reasoning` — what the student must actually do;
- `evidence_location` — paragraph, sentence, page, visual, section, or text pair;
- `evidence_distance` — immediate, local, paragraph, distributed, whole-text, or cross-text;
- `expected_answer`;
- `acceptable_evidence`;
- `reasoning_link` where the evidence-to-answer relationship is not obvious;
- `response_mode` — oral, constructed, multiple choice, annotation, sorting, or other purposeful format.

For multiple-choice questions also record:

- the correct option;
- each distractor; and
- the reading error or misconception that makes each distractor plausible but wrong.

If a question cannot be described clearly in this record, redesign it before placing it on a teacher sheet or student page.

## Review gate

Before release, review the final questions against the final text, not an earlier draft. Confirm that:

- the question measures the intended weekly or spiralled reading process;
- the answer is defensible from the supplied text or visual;
- the evidence location and evidence distance are accurate;
- wording does not create accidental difficulty unrelated to reading;
- MCQ distractors are plausible, distinct, and demonstrably wrong for text-based reasons;
- constructed-response expectations distinguish answer, evidence, and reasoning where needed;
- questions become more independent and interpretive across groups without changing the core instructional destination;
- question variety supports instruction rather than imitating a standardised test.
